\section{Introduction}

An ocean forecast is useful only relative to the cheapest thing one could have
done instead. In operational oceanography that reference is almost always
\emph{persistence}: predict that tomorrow equals today. Persistence is a
famously hard baseline on autocorrelated geophysical series, and a model that
beats it is normally taken to have learned something.

This paper argues that persistence alone cannot support that inference, and
that the way it fails is systematically misleading rather than merely noisy.

The argument is structural. Persistence error grows with the forecast horizon,
because the ocean decorrelates from its present state. The error of a
\emph{seasonal climatology} does not: knowing that it is February is exactly as
informative thirty days ahead as it is one day ahead. A skill score computed
against persistence therefore has a denominator that inflates with horizon
while the numerator of a purely seasonal model does not. The consequence is
that a model which has learned nothing beyond the annual cycle will display
skill against persistence that \emph{increases} with horizon --- precisely the
pattern a reader interprets as a model with genuine long-range predictive
content.

We encountered this pattern empirically. In a framework trained across
\NVars{} ocean variables, skill against persistence rose with horizon for
\NRising{} of them. Whether that reflected real forecast skill or a seasonal
artefact was, with persistence as the only baseline, unanswerable. This paper
resolves it by adding the baseline that discriminates, and finds that both
readings are present in the same set of models --- including
\NLoseToClim{} model--horizon combinations that beat persistence while losing
to climatology outright.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item A \textbf{two-baseline skill audit} over \NVars{} operational ocean
variables $\times$ 4 horizons $\times$ \NSites{} global sites (\NModels{}
models), scoring every model against both persistence and a
leakage-controlled day-of-year climatology. The two baselines order the same
models differently, and the disagreement is a systematic function of horizon
rather than noise.
\item A \textbf{diagnosis of the horizon--skill inversion}, separating genuine
long-range skill from a seasonal artefact, and identifying the variables for
which a climatology --- cheaper to compute and to serve --- is the rational
operational choice.
\item \textbf{Leave-one-site-out spatial validation} blocked in space and time
simultaneously (\NLosoFits{} held-out fits), testing the assumption that
licenses serving a globally pooled model at arbitrary coordinates.
\item \textbf{Negative results reported in full}, including \NLoseToPers{}
model--horizon combinations that lose to persistence outright. These identify
a class of high-inertia variables for which the approach is contraindicated.
\item A \textbf{practical decision procedure} (Fig.~\ref{fig:interpretation})
mapping the pair $(S_{\mathrm{pers}}, S_{\mathrm{clim}})$ to a deployment
recommendation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Skill scores relative to a reference forecast are standard in numerical weather
prediction, and are conventionally reported against climatology, persistence,
or both \citep{jolliffe2012,murphy1988}. The concern raised here is not that
climatology is unknown as a baseline --- it is long-established in NWP --- but
that in the recent machine-learning-for-ocean literature persistence is
frequently the sole reference, and the specific interaction between baseline
choice and forecast horizon is rarely made explicit.

Data-driven ocean and atmospheric forecasting has moved rapidly from
convolutional recurrent architectures for sea surface temperature
\citep{shi2015convlstm} to global neural weather models
\citep{pathak2022fourcastnet,lam2023graphcast}. Evaluation practice has not
kept pace uniformly: gains are often reported for a single variable at a single
horizon against a single baseline. Our contribution is orthogonal to
architecture. We deliberately hold the model class fixed --- gradient-boosted
trees, one configuration, no variable-specific code --- so that variation in
the results is attributable to the variable, the horizon and the baseline
rather than to model tuning.

The leakage discipline we adopt for the climatology follows standard practice
in ecological and environmental modelling, where fitting any component of a
baseline or label definition on the evaluation period is a known and
consequential error \citep{roberts2017cv}. Our spatial validation follows the
same literature's argument against random $k$-fold splits on spatially
autocorrelated data.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Variables and sites}

We study \NVars{} ocean variables spanning physics, surface waves and
biogeochemistry (Table~\ref{tab:variables}), all drawn from operational
products: Copernicus Marine Service analysis and forecast fields for the
physical and biogeochemical variables, and the same provider's wave products
for the wave variables. Variables were included on the criterion that a
complete \NSites{}-site history was available in our archive at the snapshot
date --- a data-availability criterion applied before any model was scored,
discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

Each variable is sampled at \NSites{} sites chosen to span the major ocean
basins and dynamical regimes: western boundary currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio,
Agulhas), eastern boundary upwelling systems (Peru, Benguela, Canary),
subtropical gyres, marginal and shelf seas, and the Southern Ocean. Site
locations are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sitemap}. The record spans 2024-06-11 to
2026-08-09 at daily resolution.

\subsection{Forecast framework}

For a target variable $y$ at horizon $h$, features at time $t$ comprise lagged
values of $y$ and its covariates at lags $\{1,3,7,14,30\}$~days; rolling mean,
standard deviation, minimum and maximum over windows of $\{7,14,30\}$~days;
first differences; linear trend coefficients over $\{7,30\}$~days; calendar and
cyclical encodings of day of year; and static site descriptors (latitude,
longitude, ocean depth, basin). All rolling windows close at $t$ inclusive,
which is safe because $h \geq 1$ is enforced. Exactly one forward shift exists
in the pipeline, in target construction.

Models are LightGBM regressors \citep{ke2017lightgbm} with fixed
hyperparameters across all variables and horizons (400 trees, learning rate
0.05, 31 leaves). No per-variable tuning was performed: tuning would confound
the cross-variable comparison that is the point of the audit.

\para{Delta targets}
Models regress on the \emph{change} $y(t+h) - y(t)$ rather than the level
$y(t+h)$. A regression tree partitions feature space into piecewise-constant
regions and therefore cannot represent the identity function
$y(t+h) = y(t)$ --- which is exactly what persistence is. Fitted on levels, the
framework scored worse than persistence at every horizon tested. Under a delta
target persistence becomes the constant zero function, which a tree represents
exactly, and the same data and features yield positive skill. Predictions are
decoded by adding the model output to the anchor $y(t)$. For circular
variables (wave direction) the target is the signed angular change wrapped to
$(-180\degree, 180\degree]$ and decoding is modulo $360\degree$.

\para{Globally pooled models}
One model per (variable, horizon) is fitted across all \NSites{} sites jointly,
with site coordinates and depth as features, rather than one model per site.
This is what allows the framework to score arbitrary coordinates rather than
only the sites it was fitted on --- and it is the assumption
Section~\ref{sec:loso} tests directly.

\subsection{Baselines}

\para{Persistence}
$\hat{y}_{\text{pers}}(t+h) = y(t)$, the last observed value at feature time.
This is also the anchor of the delta target, so a delta model that outputs
zero \emph{is} persistence exactly.

\para{Climatology}
For each site $s$ and day of year $d$, the climatology is the mean of all
training observations at that site whose day of year falls within
$\pm\ClimWindow$ days of $d$, treating the year as circular so that 31
December neighbours 1 January:
%
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{\text{clim}}(s, d) = \frac{1}{|W(d)|}\sum_{t \in W(d)} y_s(t),
\end{equation}
%
where $W(d) = \{t : \delta(\mathrm{doy}(t), d) \leq \ClimWindow\}$ and $\delta$
is circular day-of-year distance. The window is necessary
rather than cosmetic: the record is roughly 2.2 years, so a bare
(site, day-of-year) mean would average two samples and be mostly noise --- a
strawman the model would beat for the wrong reason. A $\pm\ClimWindow$-day
window pools approximately 60 samples per estimate. For circular variables the
mean is the circular mean, computed via $\operatorname{atan2}$ of the mean sine
and cosine; the arithmetic mean of $359\degree$ and $1\degree$ is $180\degree$,
which would make the baseline point in exactly the wrong direction.

\para{Leakage control}
The climatology is refitted \emph{inside every cross-validation fold} on that
fold's training rows only (shaded in Fig.~\ref{fig:dataflow}). Fitting a
climatology once over the full record would leak the evaluation period into the
baseline's own definition, flattering the baseline and understating the model
--- an error in the ``safe'' direction, and therefore one that is easy to leave
in place. Fit and application are implemented as two separate functions so that
fitting on the wrong rows requires deliberate effort, and the discipline is
enforced by a unit test that shifts the second year of a synthetic series by a
known constant and asserts the baseline's error on that period recovers the
shift.

\subsection{Validation}

Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow} shows both experiments end to end.

\para{Rolling-origin cross-validation}
Five expanding-window folds, ordered in time, with an embargo gap equal to the
forecast horizon between each training window and its test window --- a
training row within $h$ days of the test window shares its target period.
Random $k$-fold splitting is never used. The pooled multi-site frame is sorted
by time so that a fold boundary separates \emph{dates}, and no site's future
can leak into another site's past.

\para{Skill score}
For a reference forecast $r$,
%
\begin{equation}
S_r = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_{\text{model}}^2}{\mathrm{RMSE}_{r}^2},
\end{equation}
%
so $S_r > 0$ means the model beats reference $r$, $S_r = 0$ is parity, and
$S_r < 0$ means the reference is better. We report $S_{\mathrm{pers}}$ and
$S_{\mathrm{clim}}$ for every model. Where the climatology cannot answer for
some test rows (a day of year no site in the training fold observed), both
model and baseline are scored on the intersection, and the reduced count is
reported rather than the rows being silently dropped.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

All experiments run from a fixed archival snapshot (\AsOf{}), pinned so that
window boundaries --- and therefore every cached provider request --- are
identical across reruns. Without this pinning each rerun re-requests the record
from upstream providers and, because those providers intermittently return
partial responses, occasionally scores a slightly different dataset, making two
runs of the same experiment non-comparable. Every figure and table cell in this
paper is generated programmatically from the experiment output; no number is
transcribed by hand.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The two baselines disagree, and disagree with horizon}

Figure~\ref{fig:skill} is the central result. For each variable it plots skill
against persistence and skill against climatology as a function of horizon.

\begin{wfigure}[tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_skill_by_horizon.pdf}
\caption{Skill against two baselines, by forecast horizon, for each of the
\NVars{} variables. Skill against persistence (blue circles) and against
climatology (orange squares) move in \emph{opposite} directions with horizon
for most variables. The dashed line is parity; points below it mean the
baseline wins. A reader given only the blue curve would conclude these models
improve with lead time.}
\label{fig:skill}
\end{wfigure}

The two curves systematically diverge. Median skill against persistence rises
from \MedSkillPOne{} at \SI{1}{\day} to \MedSkillPThirty{} at \SI{30}{\day},
while median skill against climatology falls from \MedSkillCOne{} to
\MedSkillCThirty{} over the same range. This is the structural prediction of
Section~1 realised in data: persistence becomes easy to beat as the horizon
grows because the ocean decorrelates from its present state, while climatology
becomes \emph{harder} to beat because the seasonal cycle is an increasing share
of what remains predictable.

The practical consequence is that the two baselines license opposite statements
about the same models. Reported against persistence alone, this framework
appears to improve with lead time. Reported against climatology alone, it
appears to degrade toward the seasonal cycle. Both are true; neither is
complete. Per-variable skill scores are given in Table~\ref{tab:skill}, and the
full error decomposition in the appendix table.

\subsection{What survives the seasonal cycle}

The inversion does not mean every model is purely seasonal. At \SI{30}{\day},
skill against climatology remains positive for most variables, meaning they
carry information the seasonal cycle does not. But \NLoseToClim{}
model--horizon combinations have $S_{\mathrm{clim}} < 0$ while
$S_{\mathrm{pers}} > 0$: these models beat the last observed value and lose to
the seasonal cycle. Under the reporting convention this literature most often
uses, they would be presented as successes.

The correct reading is that long-horizon skill measured against persistence is
substantially --- and for some variables entirely --- seasonal, and only the
climatology comparison separates the two components. Variables whose
$S_{\mathrm{clim}}$ approaches or crosses zero at long range are those for
which the model has effectively reduced to a seasonal lookup, and for which a
climatology would be the rational operational choice.

\subsection{Where the framework fails}

\NLoseToPers{} model--horizon combinations have $S_{\mathrm{pers}} < 0$: the
model is worse than doing nothing. These concentrate in the high-inertia
subsurface variables, and the pattern is physically coherent. For a variable
whose autocorrelation time greatly exceeds the forecast horizon, persistence is
close to optimal and the residual the delta model is asked to predict is close
to pure noise; fitting it adds variance without adding signal. This is a
property of the variable rather than a defect of the implementation, and it is
useful precisely because it is predictable in advance from the autocorrelation
structure --- it identifies where this class of model should not be deployed at
all.

Notably these are not the same variables that fail against climatology. Some
subsurface variables lose to persistence while beating climatology by a wide
margin; some wave variables do the reverse. The two baselines fail in different
places, which is the strongest available argument for reporting both.

\subsection{Spatial transfer: leave-one-site-out}
\label{sec:loso}

Serving a globally pooled model at arbitrary coordinates rests on an assumption
that is rarely tested: that the model generalises to ocean it never saw during
training, rather than interpolating among its training sites. We test this
directly. For each variable, horizon and site, we refit on the remaining
\(\NSites - 1\) sites and score on the held-out one (\NLosoFits{} fits).

The split is blocked in space \emph{and} time. Training uses the earlier 80\%
of the record at the 23 retained sites; testing uses the later 20\% at the
held-out site. A purely spatial hold-out would leave the model trained on the
same dates it is tested on, measuring interpolation across a shared period
rather than transfer. The climatology baseline is held to the same standard: it
is fitted only on the 23 training sites, so for the held-out site it falls back
to the nearest fitted site by great-circle distance --- the spatial analogue of
what the model itself is being asked to do.

decorrelation scales that a model fitted on distant sites cannot resolve.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A decision procedure}

Figure~\ref{fig:interpretation} states the practical consequence of these
results as a procedure. The pair $(S_{\mathrm{pers}}, S_{\mathrm{clim}})$,
evaluated across more than one horizon and checked against a held-out site,
maps onto a small number of clearly distinguishable recommendations --- three
of which are ``do not deploy this model'' for reasons that a single baseline
cannot surface.

\subsection{Implications}

\para{Report both baselines}
Persistence and climatology are individually cheap and jointly diagnostic, and
their disagreement carries information neither provides alone. A model that
beats persistence at long range while losing to climatology has not learned to
forecast; it has learned what month it is. That failure mode is invisible under
a single-baseline convention, and it fails in the flattering direction.

\para{Baseline choice interacts with horizon, so single-horizon evaluation
hides it} The divergence in Fig.~\ref{fig:skill} is only visible across
horizons. A paper reporting one horizon --- however carefully --- can report a
number that is correct and still leave the reader unable to tell which of two
very different models produced it.

\para{Architecture is not the lever these results point to}
We held the model class fixed deliberately. Nothing here argues that
gradient-boosted trees are the right architecture; the results argue that
\emph{evaluation} is currently the binding constraint on interpreting such
comparisons. A more expressive architecture evaluated against persistence alone
at a single horizon would produce a number that is neither comparable to these
nor obviously more informative.

\para{Pooling across sites is defensible but should be reported as such}
The leave-one-site-out results support serving a globally pooled model at
arbitrary coordinates, with the caveat that pooled metrics overstate
performance at unseen locations, and that the overstatement is largest exactly
in the energetic regions users are most likely to care about.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\para{Record length}
The archive spans roughly 2.2 years. This is the most consequential limitation
and it bears directly on the central comparison: a climatology estimated from
two annual cycles is weakly constrained, and the $\pm\ClimWindow$-day window is
what makes it a serious baseline rather than a noisy one. A longer record would
strengthen the climatology and, we expect, \emph{reduce} the measured
$S_{\mathrm{clim}}$ --- our headline comparison is therefore conservative in
the direction that favours the model, and the qualitative conclusion is
unlikely to reverse. It also means we cannot separate the seasonal cycle from
interannual variability.

\para{Variable coverage}
The \NVars{} variables reported are those with complete \NSites{}-site coverage
in the archive at the snapshot date. Others were excluded on data availability,
not on their results. The criterion was applied before any model was scored, so
it does not select on outcome, but the set is not a random sample of ocean
variables --- it is skewed toward well-observed operational products.

\para{Reanalysis as ground truth}
The ``observations'' are operational analysis products, not in-situ
measurements. They are themselves model output constrained by assimilated
observations, so our scores measure agreement with an analysis rather than with
the ocean. This is standard for work at this spatial coverage, but it bounds
what the results can claim, particularly in sparsely observed regions where the
analysis is least constrained.

\para{Circular variables are modelled linearly}
Wave direction is decoded circularly and scored with circular error, but the
underlying regression treats the wrapped delta as a linear target: a veer of
$5\degree$ across north trains as $-355\degree$. The principled fix ---
predicting vector components and deriving the angle --- is not applied here,
and the direction results should be read with that caveat.

\para{Fixed hyperparameters}
No per-variable tuning was performed. This is necessary for the cross-variable
comparison but means every individual model is likely below its own achievable
ceiling. The audit measures the framework as configured, not the best
attainable skill per variable.

\section{Conclusion}

Skill against persistence and skill against climatology are not interchangeable
summaries of the same quantity, and the difference between them is not noise:
it is a systematic function of forecast horizon that can invert the qualitative
conclusion. Across \NVars{} ocean variables, \NSites{} sites and four horizons,
median skill against persistence rises with lead time while median skill
against climatology falls, and \NLoseToClim{} model--horizon combinations beat
persistence while losing to the seasonal cycle outright.

We also find that globally pooled models do transfer to sites they never saw,
with \LosoPositiveShare{}\% of held-out sites retaining positive skill at
\SI{7}{\day}, but at a real cost that pooled metrics conceal, concentrated in
dynamically energetic regions.

The recommendation is cheap to adopt: report both baselines, across more than
one horizon, and report the cases where the baseline wins.
