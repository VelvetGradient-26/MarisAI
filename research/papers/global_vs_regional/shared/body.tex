\section{Introduction}

A species distribution model (SDM) is conventionally improved by adding data.
More occurrence records, more environmental coverage, a wider geographic
extent --- all read, by default, as strictly more evidence for the model to
learn from. This paper reports a case where that default is wrong in a
controlled, quantified way: a fish-habitat model trained on \DataMultiple{}
times more data, drawn from the whole global ocean rather than one regional
sea, scores substantially \emph{worse} on the water the regional model was
built for, and the mechanism is not what the obvious explanation would guess.

The obvious explanation for a broad-extent model underperforming on a specific
region is extrapolation: the global model has learned an average relationship
that does not hold locally, or its predictions fall outside the range of
conditions it was actually trained on. We rule this out directly. A
Multivariate Environmental Similarity Surface (MESS) analysis
\citep{elith2010} shows the global model extrapolates \emph{less} on the
regional holdout than the regional model itself does on its own holdout
(\MESSFracGlobal{}\% of rows outside the training envelope, against
\MESSFracRegion{}\% --- see \S\ref{sec:results}). The global model has simply
seen more of the world's climate space. It still predicts worse.

The mechanism, diagnosed with SHAP feature attribution, is a shift in which
variables the model actually uses. The regional model's two leading features
are coastal geometry --- depth and distance to coast. The global model's two
leading features are water-mass chemistry --- dissolved oxygen and a 60-day
lagged temperature. Both are defensible predictors of fish presence in
principle. In practice, the local, high-resolution signal that coastal
geometry carries for this species group in this sea is displaced by
coarser-grained chemistry that generalises across oceans but says less about
any one of them. This is closer to a known machine-learning failure mode ---
\emph{negative transfer}, in which target-irrelevant source data measurably
degrades performance on the target \citep{zhang2021survey} --- appearing
inside a domain, species distribution modelling, that has largely discussed
the global-versus-regional extent question in terms of extrapolation and
sample size rather than feature-level displacement.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item A \textbf{matched-holdout controlled comparison}: a global and a
regional ensemble SDM for the same species group, evaluated on the
\emph{identical} \NHoldout{} held-out regional rows, with the regional
holdout's spatial blocks removed from the global model's training set to
prevent the global model from having already seen the water it is tested on.
\item \textbf{Comparative use of MESS} to eliminate the standard
counter-explanation --- that the broader-extent model must simply be
extrapolating more --- rather than using MESS only within a single model, as
is conventional.
\item A \textbf{SHAP-based mechanism diagnosis} showing \emph{which} features
are displaced when extent grows, connecting the result to feature-level
evidence rather than resting on the aggregate skill gap alone.
\item An explicit reconciliation with the two most directly relevant prior
findings --- that regional data resists improvement from range-wide data under
biased sampling \citep{elgabbas2018}, and that truncating background-sampling
extent produces unrepresentative predictions \citep{hanberry2026} --- showing
why this result does not contradict the second despite an apparently opposite
headline recommendation (\S\ref{sec:related}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\para{Global versus regional extent in SDM practice} The closest prior result
is El-Gabbas and Dormann's comparison of regional (Egypt-only) against
range-wide-extent Maxent models for 17 bat species and find that range-wide
data does not improve the regional, sampling-biased models, attributing the
low geographic congruence to sampling-effort bias rather than to ecological
signal. Their diagnosis uses Maxent permutation importance and finds
accessibility-bias predictors (distance to roads and cities) dominating ---
a different mechanism from the water-chemistry-versus-coastal-geometry shift
reported here, and they do not use MESS to test whether the two models
extrapolate differently. Their own discussion recommends building
\emph{larger}, multi-country models rather than smaller ones, a tension this
paper addresses directly.

Hanberry's 2026 study \citep{hanberry2026} is the paper most likely to be read as contradicting this
result, and needs its distinction stated explicitly rather than left implicit.
Hanberry manipulates the sampling extent of \emph{both} presence and
background/absence samples --- truncated to small ecological units versus the
full North American continent --- for 14 wide-ranging tree species, and finds
that truncating both classes to a small extent removes the climate contrast a
classifier needs to separate them, producing unconstrained, unrepresentative
predictions. The recommendation to ``model bigger'' is about not truncating
background-sampling extent when the goal is to represent a species' full
range. That is a different question from the one asked here on three
structural points: Hanberry's untruncated model is evaluated against the full
continental range, not against an identical holdout shared with the truncated
models; his absences are plain random background points, which is exactly the
sampling design his own climate-contrast failure depends on, where this
work's models (both regional and global) use target-group background,
designed to avoid that specific artefact; and his mechanism is climate-contrast
collapse from an \emph{under-sampled} extent, which a comparative MESS
analysis rules out here --- the global model in this study extrapolates
\emph{less}, not more, than the regional one. This paper expands the training
extent and holds the evaluation region fixed; Hanberry truncates the training
extent and lets the evaluation region grow with it. Growing one does not
predict what happens under the other.

\para{Transferability and niche truncation} A broader literature establishes
that SDM transferability depends more on environmental distance than
geographic distance, and that the relationship between extent and performance
is not monotonic \citep{seuren2025,gantchoff2022}. Guisan et al.\ \citep{guisan2025} frame
the general tension --- a whole-range model risks niche truncation, a
subrange model risks losing generality --- and propose spatially nested SDMs
(N-SDM) as the field's compromise: a coarse whole-range model combined with a
fine subrange model. This result is complementary to, not contradicted by,
that proposal: it is evidence for why the subrange half of an N-SDM is worth
keeping, since the coarse half measurably loses local skill on exactly the
water this platform serves.

\para{The same model class, without an extent comparison} Yang et al.\ \citep{yang2025}
apply LightGBM with SHAP to yellowfin tuna distribution in the western and
central Pacific --- the same species family, the same model class, and the
same interpretability tool used here --- but at a single spatial extent, with
no global-versus-regional comparison. Their own stated future work calls
explicitly for ``comparative analysis across diverse fleets and fishing
regions,'' which this paper's design directly answers, with a species group
that includes yellowfin tuna in a different ocean basin.

\para{Negative transfer} The general machine-learning phenomenon of additional
source data degrading target-domain performance is established outside
ecology \citep{zhang2021survey}, including the specific observation that an
overabundance of target-irrelevant data exacerbates source/target divergence
--- closely matching the mechanism reported here, where \NGlobalTrain{}
weakly-relevant global rows dilute the locally relevant coastal-geometry
signal that \NRegionalTrain{} regional rows preserve. No prior source connects
this literature to an ecological SDM extent-choice debate through a
comparative MESS-plus-SHAP diagnosis.

\section{Methods}

\para{Study system} The species group is five species relevant to Indian
Ocean fisheries, modelled as habitat suitability (presence/background) with a
three-member ensemble --- Maxent, random forest, and LightGBM, combined by a
softmax-weighted vote over cross-validated true skill statistic (TSS). The
regional extent is the North Indian Ocean box this platform serves; the
global extent is the worldwide ocean, coarsened and cached under the same
feature-store pipeline (\S\S ``Machine learning conventions'' and ``Going
global,'' internal documentation).

\para{Matched-holdout construction} Comparing each model's own reported
cross-validated skill is not a valid comparison, because the two models are
scored on different water by construction: the global model's own holdout is
overwhelmingly non-regional. A naive comparison of that kind favours the
global model on this dataset (LightGBM CV TSS \CVTSSGlobalLGBM{} global versus
\CVTSSRegionLGBM{} regional), the opposite of what a matched comparison finds.
Two further hazards would each independently invalidate an unmatched
comparison. First, the regional and global models' own shipped holdouts are
different spatial blocks of different feature stores, so two numbers from two
different bodies of water are not comparable. Second, the shipped global
model was trained on every ocean, including the region under test, so scoring
it on the regional holdout as-is would score it on water it has already seen
--- an upper bound with the answer written into the training set, not a
generalisation test.

The comparison is therefore built as follows. The regional model's own final
holdout block is reproduced exactly (spatial block cross-validation, blocks
of \(3\deg\), five splits, the first split at the model's original seed). The
\emph{spatial blocks} containing any holdout row --- not merely the matching
rows --- are then removed from the global feature store, because a global
point 50 km from a held-out regional point is the same water under a
different label; block removal, not row removal, is why spatial-block
cross-validation exists in the first place. A fresh global ensemble is then
fit on the reduced global store, using the same feature columns, the same
ensemble-fitting routine, and the same softmax-over-CV-TSS weighting rule as
the shipped model, so the weighting itself is not a second, uncontrolled
variable. Both the reproduced regional model and the freshly fit global model
are then scored on the identical \NHoldout{} regional holdout rows
(\NHoldoutPos{} presences).

\para{Extrapolation diagnosis} A MESS score \citep{elith2010} is computed for
every holdout row against each model's own training envelope, giving both a
per-row similarity value and the fraction of rows falling outside that
envelope (negative MESS). This is the same tool used conventionally within a
single model to flag novel conditions; here it is used comparatively, between
two models of differing training extent, specifically to test whether a
skill gap can be explained by one model seeing less-similar conditions than
the other.

\para{Mechanism diagnosis} SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values are
computed for the LightGBM tier of each ensemble over its own training data,
and mean absolute SHAP is ranked per feature, giving a feature-importance
ordering for the regional and global models independently.

The regional ensemble outperforms the global ensemble on every accuracy
metric evaluated, on the same rows: TSS \TSSRegion{} against \TSSGlobal{},
Boyce index \BoyceRegion{} against \BoyceGlobal{}, ROC-AUC \ROCRegion{}
against \ROCGlobal{}, PR-AUC \PRRegion{} against \PRGlobal{}. The gap holds
for every individual tier of the ensemble, not only the combined vote:
LightGBM alone scores TSS \TSSRegionLGBM{} regional against \TSSGlobalLGBM{}
global; random forest \TSSRegionRF{} against \TSSGlobalRF{}; Maxent
\TSSRegionMaxent{} against \TSSGlobalMaxent{}. This is the opposite ranking
from each model's own within-store cross-validated TSS
(\CVTSSRegionLGBM{}--\CVTSSGlobalLGBM{} for LightGBM,
\CVTSSRegionRF{}--\CVTSSGlobalRF{} for random forest), which is exactly the
comparison a reader would reach for absent a matched holdout, and which
reports the opposite conclusion.

\para{Extrapolation is not the explanation} The global model's holdout rows
have a lower fraction outside its training envelope than the regional model's
holdout rows have outside its own (\MESSFracGlobal{}\% against
\MESSFracRegion{}\%), even though the global model scores worse. Median
per-row similarity is lower for the global model (\MESSMedGlobal{} against
\MESSMedRegion{}), consistent with a much larger and more heterogeneous
training envelope in which any single region sits less centrally --- but
``sits less centrally in a much larger, well-covered space'' is not the same
claim as ``is extrapolating,'' and the extrapolation fraction is the metric
that speaks to the latter. The skill gap cannot be attributed to the global
model encountering unfamiliar conditions.

\para{The mechanism is feature displacement} The regional ensemble's two
leading SHAP features are \texttt{depth} (mean $|\mathrm{SHAP}|$
\SHAPRegionDepth{}) and \texttt{distance\_to\_coast} (\SHAPRegionCoast{}) ---
coastal geometry, at high resolution, specific to the shelf and coastline
this species group actually occupies. The global ensemble's two leading
features are \texttt{o2}, dissolved oxygen (\SHAPGlobalOTwo{}), and a 60-day
lagged temperature, \texttt{thetao\_lag60} (\SHAPGlobalLagSixty{}); depth and
distance to coast fall to fourth and ninth by importance
(\SHAPGlobalDepth{}, \SHAPGlobalCoast{}). The global model has not failed to
learn a relationship; it has learned a different, coarser one, driven by
variables that vary meaningfully across the whole training population but
carry comparatively little of the locally decisive signal for this specific
water.

\para{Ablation: the displaced features are causally implicated, not merely
associated} SHAP importance shows what the global model relies on; it does
not by itself show that relying on \texttt{o2} and \texttt{thetao\_lag60}
\emph{costs} regional skill, as opposed to those features simply being
correlated with whatever does. We test this directly by refitting the
global model's LightGBM tier with both features removed and rescoring it on
the identical regional holdout. Removing them improves every metric: TSS
\AblFullTSS{} \(\rightarrow\) \AblAblatedTSS{}, Boyce
\AblFullBoyce{} \(\rightarrow\) \AblAblatedBoyce{}, ROC-AUC
\AblFullROC{} \(\rightarrow\) \AblAblatedROC{}, PR-AUC
\AblFullPR{} \(\rightarrow\) \AblAblatedPR{}. The direction confirms the
mechanism: these two features are not innocent bystanders of a larger
population, they measurably cost regional skill when present.

The improvement is real but partial. Against the regional LightGBM tier's
own TSS on this holdout (\TSSRegionLGBM{}), removing \texttt{o2} and
\texttt{thetao\_lag60} closes only \AblGapClosedPct{}\% of the gap. Two of
forty features being causally implicated but not sufficient is consistent
with the dilution account in \S\ref{sec:results}: \texttt{thermal\_position}
also ranks far higher for the global model than the regional one
(Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}) and was left untouched by this ablation, and a
model relying on \emph{any} subset of coarse, globally-common variables in
place of the fine, locally-decisive ones would show the same qualitative
signature without the specific two features named here being the whole
story. The claim this paper makes is therefore precise: feature
displacement is a demonstrated, causally confirmed contributor to the
regional-holdout gap, not a fully sufficient explanation of its magnitude.

\section{Discussion}

Three findings, taken together, rule out the two most obvious explanations
for the skill gap and leave a third. It is not that the global model has too
little data (it has \DataMultiple{}$\times$ more). It is not that the global
model is extrapolating outside familiar conditions (comparative MESS shows
the opposite). What is left is that the composition of the added data
changes which features the model relies on, and the features favoured by a
much larger, more heterogeneous population are not the features that carry
the most decisive local signal. This is a dilution effect on feature
relevance, not a coverage effect on the input space, and the two are
distinguishable exactly because MESS and SHAP answer different questions ---
one about where a point falls in the training distribution, the other about
what the model does with the features it is given.

\para{Reconciling with the literature that recommends going bigger} Both
El-Gabbas and Dormann's finding \citep{elgabbas2018} and Hanberry's \citep{hanberry2026} are compatible with this
result once the specific claim each makes is kept separate from the
headline. El-Gabbas and Dormann's regional models do not improve under
range-wide data because of \emph{sampling bias}, and their own recommendation
to go bigger is offered as future work, not demonstrated in their study.
Hanberry's recommendation to model bigger addresses \emph{background-sampling
extent}, a variable this paper does not touch --- both the regional and
global ensembles here draw target-group background at their respective
extents, not truncated random background, which is the specific setting his
climate-contrast failure requires. Read together, the emerging picture is not
``bigger is always better'' or ``smaller is always better,'' but that the
\emph{axis being varied} matters: truncating background-sampling extent
degrades representativeness (Hanberry); adding weakly-relevant occurrence
volume from outside the region of interest degrades skill on that region
(this paper); and whether range-wide occurrence data helps a regional model
at all depends on whether that data corrects or worsens the regional model's
own sampling bias (El-Gabbas and Dormann). These are three different
variables, not three attempts to answer the same question with different
answers.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

The result is demonstrated for one species group, one ocean, and one model
architecture (a three-member ensemble headed by LightGBM); Yang et al.'s
\citep{yang2025} own call for cross-regional comparison of the same architecture on the same
species family is a natural second instance, and a second region entirely
would turn this from one controlled instance into a claim about scale. The
mechanism diagnosis uses SHAP on the LightGBM tier only, not the ensemble as
a whole or the other two tiers individually; a per-tier SHAP comparison is a
bounded follow-on, and the ablation in \S\ref{sec:results} is itself
LightGBM-only for the same reason. That ablation confirms the displacement
mechanism causally rather than leaving it as a plausible SHAP-only reading,
but it also shows the mechanism is not complete: removing the two named
features closes only \AblGapClosedPct{}\% of the LightGBM-tier gap. The
natural next experiment is therefore not whether feature displacement
matters --- that is now shown directly --- but how much of the remaining gap
a broader ablation closes: repeating it over every feature that ranks
higher for the global model than the regional one (starting with
\texttt{thermal\_position}, the next-largest single displacement in
Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}), and over the random-forest and Maxent tiers, which
this ablation does not touch.

\section{Conclusion}

Expanding a regional fish-habitat model's training extent to the whole global
ocean multiplies its training data \DataMultiple{}$\times$ without extending
the usable time window, and on the region the model was built to serve,
degrades every accuracy metric measured despite extrapolating less, not more,
than the regional model does on its own water. The mechanism is a shift in
which features the model relies on: coastal geometry, displaced by water-mass
chemistry. The result does not contradict prior findings that recommend
larger training extents; it identifies the specific axis --- added
weakly-relevant occurrence volume, holding evaluation region fixed --- along
which more data measurably hurts, distinct from the axes those findings
address.
