Forecast verification · 13 ocean variables · 24 sites

Rising skill, falling skill

Ocean forecasts are almost always scored against persistence. That single baseline fails in the one direction that flatters the model — and the failure grows with forecast horizon, which is exactly where people read success.

Deepak Krishna · MarisAI · snapshot 2026-08-10

The argument

Persistence — predict that tomorrow equals today — is a hard baseline on autocorrelated geophysical series, and beating it is normally read as evidence that a model learned something. The problem is structural rather than statistical.

Persistence error grows with the forecast horizon, because the ocean decorrelates from its present state. The error of a seasonal climatology does not: knowing that it is February is exactly as informative thirty days ahead as it is one day ahead. A skill score against persistence therefore has a denominator that inflates with horizon — which holds the score up even as the model's own error grows.

A model that has learned nothing but the time of year can hold its skill against persistence flat, or increase it, while genuinely degrading. Flat skill with lead time is what a reader takes for long-range predictive content.

We hit this pattern in a working forecasting engine and could not tell, with persistence as the only baseline, whether it was real skill or a seasonal artefact. This work adds the baseline that discriminates, and audits 52 models to find out.

How the framework is built

One acquisition path, one feature builder and one model class serve every variable; nothing in the pipeline branches on which variable it is training. That uniformity is what makes a cross-variable audit meaningful — differences in the results cannot be blamed on differences in the implementation.

Why the model predicts change, not level

A regression tree partitions feature space into piecewise-constant regions, so it cannot represent the identity function y(t+h) = y(t) — which is exactly what persistence is. Fitted on levels, the framework scored worse than persistence at every horizon. Fitted on the change, persistence becomes the constant zero function, which a tree represents exactly, and the same data and features yield positive skill.

How the experiments are run

The climatology is a per-site day-of-year mean over a ±15-day circular window, and it is refitted inside every cross-validation fold on that fold's training rows only. Fitting it once over the whole record would leak the evaluation period into the baseline's own definition — flattering the baseline, understating the model. That is an error in the safe direction, which is why it is easy to leave in place.

The window is not cosmetic. The record spans about 2.2 years, so a bare day-of-year mean would average two samples and be mostly noise — a strawman the model beats for the wrong reason. A ±15-day window pools roughly 60 samples per estimate, which is what makes this a baseline worth losing to. A longer record would strengthen the climatology further, so this comparison is conservative in the direction that favours the model.

Results

Median skill against persistence is 0.272 at one day and 0.273 at thirty — essentially unchanged, having dipped to 0.154 and 0.151 in between. Read alone, that says the models are as good at a month as at a day, which for a forecasting system would be remarkable.

Over exactly the same range, median skill against climatology falls monotonically: 0.958, 0.729, 0.410, -0.023. It declines with horizon for 13 of 13 variables without exception. By thirty days the median model has fallen below the seasonal cycle, and 7 of 13 variables lose to it outright — against 1 that lose to persistence.

The sharpest cases are the 11 model–horizon combinations that beat persistence while losing to climatology. Under a single-baseline convention every one of them reads as a success, when the correct call is to ship the climatology instead: cheaper to compute, cheaper to serve, and more accurate.

The two baselines fail in different places

5 model–horizon combinations are worse than doing nothing at all. These concentrate in high-inertia subsurface variables, and the pattern is physically coherent: when a variable's autocorrelation time greatly exceeds the horizon, persistence is near-optimal and the residual is close to pure noise, so fitting it adds variance without adding signal.

Crucially these are not the same variables that fail against climatology. Some subsurface variables lose to persistence while beating climatology by a wide margin; some wave variables do the reverse. That the two baselines fail in different places is the strongest available argument for reporting both.

Does it transfer to ocean it never saw?

Serving one globally pooled model at arbitrary coordinates rests on an assumption that is rarely tested. We tested it directly: 1248 fits, each training on 23 sites and scoring the 24th, blocked in space and time — the model trains on the earlier 80% of the record at the retained sites and is scored on the later 20% at the held-out one.

0.151 median skill at sites seen in training (7 d)
0.050 median skill at held-out sites (7 d)
56% held-out sites that keep positive skill
1248 held-out fits

Transfer is real but it costs skill, and the cost is concentrated in dynamically energetic regions — western boundary currents and upwelling systems, whose variability is driven by mesoscale processes a model fitted on distant sites cannot resolve. The majority staying positive supports serving the pooled model at unseen coordinates; the falling median means the pooled figure overstates what a user clicking on open ocean should expect, and a deployed system should say so.

What to do with two numbers

Taken together the pair maps onto a small set of clearly distinguishable recommendations — three of which are “do not deploy this model” for reasons a single baseline cannot surface.

Reproducing it

Four notebooks run the whole study end to end, in order. They need no credentials and no network — the cleaned observations they read are committed to research/data/. They import the shipped implementation rather than reimplementing it, so what they demonstrate is what the experiments ran.

01_data_and_edathe variables, sites and coverage; seasonal structure; autocorrelation, which identifies where persistence will be hard to beat
02_feature_engineeringthe ~59 features; cyclical calendar encoding; the leakage checks; and the delta-vs-level comparison the framework turns on
03_baselines_and_modelsboth baselines; why the climatology window is ±15 days; the circular mean; and a direct measurement of what fitting a baseline on the evaluation period costs
04_results_and_transferall 52 models against both baselines; the flattered cases; the outright failures; and the held-out-site results

What this does not claim

The record is about 2.2 years, so the seasonal cycle cannot be separated from interannual variability. The “observations” are operational analysis products — themselves model output constrained by assimilated data — so these scores measure agreement with an analysis, not with the ocean. The 13 variables are those with complete 24-site coverage in the archive at the snapshot date, a criterion applied before any model was scored. Hyperparameters are fixed across all variables, so no individual model is at its own ceiling. And wave direction is decoded circularly but still regressed linearly, so a veer across north trains as a near-full reversal.
